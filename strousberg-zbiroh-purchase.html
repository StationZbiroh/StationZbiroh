Stanice Zbiroh · Property and finance

How Strousberg bought Zbiroh

The 1868 transaction was not a simple cash purchase directly from the Austrian state. The state first sold the Zbiroh estate to Samuel Simundt and Vinzenz Kirchmayer. They resold it to Bethel Henry Strousberg five months later at a substantially higher price, financed through cash instalments and the assumption of an existing mortgage.

Documented outline: the Habsburg state used Zbiroh as security for borrowing in 1866, authorised its sale in 1868, and sold it to two private purchasers for 6.5 million gulden. Strousberg then agreed to pay those purchasers 9.4 million gulden: 6.4 million in cash instalments and 3 million by assuming the mortgage. Calling this a “mortgaged purchase” is therefore broadly understandable, but the more precise description is an instalment purchase combined with an assumed mortgage.

The transaction, step by step

April 1866

The state pledges Zbiroh as security

Facing serious financial pressure, the Austrian state concluded a loan of 60 million gulden with the Allgemeine Österreichische Boden-Credit-Anstalt. Several Crown or chamber properties were pledged as security, including the Zbiroh estate. The Austro-Prussian War followed, and the August 1866 peace settlement required Austria to pay Prussia 30 million gulden in reparations.

20 June 1868

Sale authorised

A law authorising the sale of the Zbiroh estate was signed. The property comprised the component estates of Zbiroh, Točník, Mirošov and Osek.

21 July 1868

Simundt and Kirchmayer buy from the state

Samuel Simundt, described as a chamber councillor in Berlin, and Vinzenz Kirchmayer, a banker and president of the Kraków Chamber of Commerce, purchased the estate for 6.5 million gulden.

31 December 1868

Strousberg buys from the two purchasers

Simundt and Kirchmayer resold the estate to Bethel Henry Strousberg for 9.4 million gulden, an increase of 2.9 million gulden over their July purchase price.

The source says that Strousberg initially paid regularly, but encountered financial difficulty around 1870–1871 during his Romanian railway project. The exact deposit, instalment dates and annual payment amounts are not established by the digitised source used here; the original sale contract and mortgage instrument are still required.

Why the Austrian state sold

Zbiroh had been a state or chamber estate rather than newly “inherited” property. During the 1860s the Habsburg monarchy began disposing of some Crown assets because their operation and modernisation were complex and expensive. Its wider fiscal pressure—state borrowing, the 1866 war and the resulting reparations—formed the immediate background to the sale.

The sale therefore belongs to a broader movement from state or chamber ownership into private capitalist ownership. It gave Strousberg control of a very large landed and industrial resource base: forests, mineral resources, established ironworks and the room to construct new plants and transport links.

What the sources do—and do not—show about the intermediaries

The documented purchasers were Simundt and Kirchmayer. Strousberg later wrote that the German purchaser was the longstanding business associate of a Berlin banker to whom Strousberg himself was closely connected and heavily indebted; he portrayed his purchase of Zbiroh as a way to rescue that friend. This is Strousberg’s retrospective account and does not identify a complete, independently verified chain of obligations.

Important distinction: the evidence presently available does not justify describing Simundt and Kirchmayer as “Goldschmidt & Kirchmayer,” nor does it prove that both men had previously financed Strousberg directly. The Vienna firm H. v. Goldschmidt & Co. appears in the separate 1875 refinancing history and should not be merged with the 1868 purchasers without a connecting document.

Why this matters for Borek

This transaction explains the legal and financial foundation from which Strousberg developed the Zbiroh industrial system. It does not yet prove that every later factory parcel—including all of the Borek works—remained subject to the same mortgage. Later cadastral and auction evidence suggests that landed-estate and industrial-property histories must be reconstructed separately.
