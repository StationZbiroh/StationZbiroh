How this page works

The history is presented as one chronological timeline. Ownership, railway operation, changes to the surrounding landscape and the later fate of the building are placed together so that each period can be understood in context. Where the evidence does not yet establish an exact date, institutional transition or architectural authorship, the gap is explicitly marked as an open research question.

Chronological timeline

1862 and the later 19th century

The railway site predates the present station building

The Prague–Plzeň railway belongs to an earlier phase of the site's history than the monumental station building of 1911–1912. Local municipal histories associate railway activity in this area with the opening of the line in 1862, but their accounts differ over the precise chronology and status of the early halt. They are therefore treated here as useful secondary leads rather than conclusive evidence for the exact form of the station in 1862.

There is, however, stronger physical and heritage evidence for a nineteenth-century layer within the protected station complex. The Památkový katalog identifies the former technical-administration building as railway-service accommodation probably built after the middle of the nineteenth century and later altered during the Secession-era reconstruction. The heritage documentation also records it on a reambulated Stable Cadastre map from the period 1869–1881.

This means that the protected complex is not simply a 1911–1912 creation: at least one surviving component is considered likely to preserve an earlier nineteenth-century railway phase. Its exact construction date, original function and relationship to the earliest halt remain open questions.

Evidence-led conclusion · nineteenth-century component probable; exact 1862 arrangement unresolved
October 1911

A State Railways Directorate project — authorship still under investigation

Heritage documentation identifies the project for the new station with the Ředitelství státních drah v Praze (State Railways Directorate in Prague), dated October 1911. It identifies the Prague builder Vendelín Hron in connection with construction. The surviving heritage description does not name an individual architect.

Jan Heindl (1872–1945) is nevertheless an important lead. He was a railway and civil architect and, as a revident of the Imperial-Royal State Railways, prepared the 1911 design for the new Praha–Královské Vinohrady railway building. A scholarly study also documents Heindl and Hron together on a 1912 geometric-Secession building in Prague, whose plans were signed by architect Jan Heindl and Vendelín Hron's construction enterprise.

These facts place both Heindl and Hron within the same State Railways-era architectural and construction milieu at precisely the period in which Zbiroh station was designed and built. They make Heindl a plausible person to investigate in relation to Zbiroh, but they do not establish that he designed this station. The archive therefore records Hron as the documented builder and Heindl as a possible architectural lead pending discovery of the original signed Zbiroh plans or equivalent primary evidence.

Open research question · who was the individual architect?
1911–1912

The present monumental station complex is built

The new Zbiroh station was constructed in the late Secession style during the doubling and reconstruction of the Prague–Plzeň railway. The main dispatch/residential building and former laundry belong to this phase; the older technical-administration building was incorporated and altered. Although named for Zbiroh, the complex stands in the cadastral territory of Kařez.

1913

Passenger life in the new station

A railway account published when passenger operation ended states that Zbiroh station had handled passengers from 1913. This provides a useful working date for passenger use associated with the present monumental station building.

Early 20th century · exact postcard date under research

Station, Hotel Borek and the earlier landscape

The postcard above preserves the physical relationship between Hotel Borek, the station and the surrounding countryside. The foreground appears low-lying and wet, while the slopes are substantially more open and sparsely wooded than today. The image establishes the visual difference; it does not establish why the tree cover was different.

1918 onward

From the Habsburg state railway system into Czechoslovakia

The political and railway administration surrounding the station changed with the creation of Czechoslovakia. The archive has not yet assembled the complete primary-document ownership and administrative chain for the building through the successive state-railway institutions.

Open research question · exact institutional ownership chain
20th century

A working railway station

For most of the twentieth century the building continued in railway use. Photographs, plans, staffing records, alterations and other evidence will be inserted into this part of the timeline as they are identified and dated.

October 1992

Construction of the D5 motorway begins nearby

Published D5 construction history records the start of work on the Bavoryně–Mýto section in October 1992. The motorway works would substantially transform the older landscape around the station.

26 October 1995

The D5 opens through the area

The Bavoryně–Kařez and Kařez–Mýto sections of the D5 were both opened to traffic on 26 October 1995. Comparison with the early postcard provides a particularly clear illustration of the transformation of the station landscape. The precise relationship between the wet ground visible in the postcard and the later motorway footprint remains under investigation.

By 2012

České dráhy owns the station building

Contemporary reporting identifies České dráhy (Czech Railways) as the owner of the station building when its railway function was coming to an end. The archive still needs primary records documenting each institutional transfer between the original state-railway period and České dráhy.

Night of 27–28 April 2012

Passenger operation at Zbiroh ends

Passenger operation at Zbiroh ended during the night of 27–28 April 2012. The new Kařez stop entered service on 28 April and passenger trains transferred there. An SŽDC project press release identifies the first train stopping at Kařez as R 828 at 00:34. Contemporary railway reporting described the change as the end of an era for a station that had handled passengers since 1913 — almost a century.

2012

The redundant building is prepared for sale

Contemporary reports from April and July 2012 state that České dráhy intended to dispose of the now-redundant station property. The historic building had become separated from the main passenger function during the railway-corridor reconstruction.

After 2012

From railway property to a new life

The next stage of the timeline concerns the sale of the station, subsequent private ownership, preservation of the protected building and the changing relationship between the former station complex and the operational railway. Exact transfer dates and documentary evidence will be added as they are established.

Present

A historical building and research archive

The former station survives as a physical record of more than a century of change in railway, industrial and landscape history. This timeline will continue to expand as primary documents, photographs and ownership records are located.

Who designed Zbiroh station?

What is established: the October 1911 project is attributed to the State Railways Directorate in Prague, and Vendelín Hron is identified as the builder.

What is not yet established: the name of the individual architect responsible for the architectural design.

Why Jan Heindl is being investigated: Heindl was a railway and civil architect working for the Imperial-Royal State Railways. In 1911 he designed the new railway building at Praha–Královské Vinohrady, and surviving research documents him collaborating with Vendelín Hron on another building in 1912. Heindl's documented dates are 11 July 1872–17 April 1945.

The next evidential step is therefore specific: locate the original October 1911 Zbiroh station drawings and examine their title blocks, signatures and approval stamps. Until such evidence is found, the archive will not attribute the design to Heindl.

Source-image archive

Historical station photographs and postcards are being assembled in a dedicated source-image collection. Images will be presented with dates and provenance where known, and uncertain dating will be identified as such.

Sources and evidence

  1. Památkový katalog / heritage record, Zbiroh railway station. Basis for the 1911–1912 construction date, late-Secession classification, State Railways Directorate in Prague project of October 1911, builder Vendelín Hron, and the identification of the protected former technical-administration building as probably originating after the middle of the nineteenth century and later altered during the Secession reconstruction. Památkový katalog; see also Atlas české architektury.
  2. Jiří E. Svoboda, Praha stoletá XXVII/2011. Architectural study documenting Jan Heindl as a revident of the Imperial-Royal State Railways, his design work on the Vinohrady railway building, and a 1912 Prague building whose plans were signed by Jan Heindl and Vendelín Hron: K autorství některých pražských fasád období secese, moderny a kubismu.
  3. National Archives of the Czech Republic, biographical list of railway figures. Identifies Jan Heindl as a railway and civil architect, born 11 July 1872 in Dvůr Králové nad Labem and died 17 April 1945 in Prague 8: biographical list.
  4. Kařez and Zbiroh municipal/local-history publications. Useful secondary evidence for the early railway chronology, including traditions concerning the early halt and Hotel Borek. Because these accounts are retrospective and are not fully consistent with one another, this archive uses them as research leads rather than treating their precise dates and descriptions as primary proof.
  5. České dálnice, D5 construction history. Records commencement of the Bavoryně–Mýto construction in October 1992 and opening of both Bavoryně–Kařez and Kařez–Mýto on 26 October 1995: Dálnice D5.
  6. SŽDC press release, 28 April 2012. Contemporary project source for the opening of Kařez on 28 April 2012 and the first stopping train, R 828 at 00:34: press release copy.
  7. Český rozhlas Plzeň, 27 April 2012. Contemporary report that Zbiroh was closing to passengers and being replaced by Kařez; it also reports that České dráhy would offer the redundant station building for sale: Nádraží ve Zbirohu nahradí zastávka Kařez.
  8. ŽelPage, 2 May 2012. Contemporary railway account recording the midnight 27/28 April transfer of passenger stops to Kařez and stating that Zbiroh had handled passengers since 1913: Nová zastávka Kařez již slouží cestujícím.
  9. ČT24, 5 April 2012. Contemporary report identifying České dráhy as preparing to sell the Zbiroh station building after trains ceased stopping there: ČD prodávají budovy, zbaví se i celého nádraží.
  10. iDNES, 24 July 2012. Identifies České dráhy as the then owner and seller of the former Zbiroh station building and associated land: Dráhy rozprodávají majetek.
  11. Stanice Zbiroh Historical Research Archive postcard. Original historical postcard captioned Zbiroh. Nové nádraží. Hotel Borek., reproduced on this page. It is the primary visual source for the early relationship of the station, hotel and surrounding landscape. Exact postcard date remains under research.

Research principle: where these sources disagree, are retrospective, are imprecise, or do not establish a point, the page treats the matter as unresolved rather than silently reconciling it.

Contribute evidence

If you have historical photographs, postcards, plans, railway documents, ownership records, architectural drawings, personal recollections or other material relating to Zbiroh station, Hotel Borek or the surrounding railway landscape, we would be interested in hearing from you. In particular, we are seeking the original 1911 station plans that could identify the individual architect and earlier records that could date the nineteenth-century technical-administration building. Evidence that corrects or challenges the chronology is equally welcome.
