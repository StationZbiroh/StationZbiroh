Stanice Zbiroh

A chronological, evidence-led history of the former Zbiroh railway station: its industrial setting, construction, ownership, passenger life, changing landscape, closure and survival.

Digitally enhanced archival view of Zbiroh station and Hotel Borek, derived from the original historical postcard preserved below.

How this page works

The history is presented as one chronological timeline. Ownership, railway operation, changes to the surrounding landscape and the later fate of the building are placed together so that each period can be understood in context. Where evidence differs — including the dating of the early railway site and the architectural authorship of the 1911 project — the competing evidence is shown rather than silently reconciled.

The Brandeis–Eisenschimmel factory and the central station question

The substantial Brandeis–Eisenschimmel industrial works at Borek belongs within the history of Zbiroh station, not merely in a separate factory history. The surviving company catalogue records a broad engineering and agricultural-machinery enterprise and identifies Zbiroh station as a logistical connection. The factory, Hotel Borek, worker settlement and railway therefore formed parts of the same local industrial and transport landscape.

The archive’s central working hypothesis is that the scale and economic importance of the Borek factory may help explain why such a substantial station complex was built and maintained here. This is historically plausible and is a principal direction of the research. It is not yet a proven construction motive: the archive has not located the original railway planning, traffic or commercial records explicitly stating that the 1911–1913 station project was commissioned because of the factory.

Chronological timeline

1862

The Prague–Plzeň railway establishes the earlier railway landscape

The railway history of this place predates the monumental station building by roughly half a century. Retrospective local accounts connect the site with the opening of the Prague–Plzeň railway in 1862, but they differ over the precise status and chronology of the early stopping or loading facilities. The archive therefore treats the exact 1862 arrangement as unresolved rather than reconstructing it from later municipal narratives.

Context established · exact local arrangement still under research
After the mid-19th century · documented by 1869–1881 map

A nineteenth-century railway building survives inside the protected complex

The heritage record provides stronger evidence than the later local narratives for an early physical layer. The former technical-administration building is described as railway-service accommodation probably built after the middle of the nineteenth century and later altered during the Secession reconstruction. Heritage documentation records the building on a reambulated Stable Cadastre map from the period 1869–1881.

This is important to the interpretation of the monument: today's protected complex is not entirely a creation of 1911–1912. It incorporates a probable nineteenth-century railway building whose exact construction date and earliest function remain to be established.

Heritage evidence · nineteenth-century component probable
1868–1875

Railway and industry develop together around Borek

The older railway layer also belongs to the period in which Henry Bethel von Strousberg was developing his industrial and railway schemes around Zbiroh and Borek. Heritage documentation for Strousberg's separate railway project dates its development to 1868–1875 and describes its intended connection with industrial works in the region. The older technical-administration building stood beside the Borek industrial landscape, but the evidence currently available does not establish that Strousberg built or owned this particular station building.

Historical context · direct Strousberg connection to the surviving building unproven
1 March 1875

The Goldschmidt report values Strousberg’s Zbirow industrial system

Chief engineer Franz Rziha signed a detailed engineering and financial assessment in Vienna for H. v. Goldschmidt & Co. The report treated Zbirow as an integrated system of mines, furnaces, rolling works, sawmills, wagon production, housing and industrial railways, and expressly included the puddling and rolling works at Borek. Goldschmidt’s proposed role was financial: valuation, refinancing, completion capital, working capital and commercial representation.

This is a direct documentary connection between the Borek works and the crisis surrounding Strousberg’s wider enterprise. It is not evidence that H. v. Goldschmidt & Co. inherited or acquired the factory, nor does similarity of surname establish a relationship with the later Goldschmied family. Read the 1875 report and its place in Strousberg’s memoir.

Primary report · financial relationship documented · ownership succession unproven
1875

The Borek works pass through auction after Strousberg’s collapse

A later industrial history states that the Borek factory was purchased at auction by Mančík and Šlesinger. The original auction record has not yet been examined by this archive, so the named transaction is retained as a sourced secondary reconstruction rather than treated as a completed title record.

The event matters to the station history because it marks a change in the industrial ownership beside the railway while the transport infrastructure remained in place.

Secondary historical account · original auction and land records sought
1879

Goldschmied and Roubíček appear in property evidence beside the railway

Land-title/property material near Zbiroh station names Emil Goldschmied, Ludwig Goldschmied and Jakob Roubíček. Family research makes Emil approximately 16 and Ludwig approximately 23–24 at the time. Their appearance creates an important possible bridge between Strousberg’s collapse and the later Roubíček–Brandeis period, but the document’s exact legal effect is still being established.

The archive is specifically testing whether the brothers received an inherited or successor interest in the Borek property and whether Roubíček acted for one or both of them because of age or legal capacity. At present, the record does not prove either proposition: Ludwig’s age in particular means that the applicable 1879 law and the document’s original wording must be checked. Examine the 1879 evidence and open questions.

Primary property evidence under study · inheritance and representation not yet proved
1879

The future Hotel Borek site is visible beside the railway

Cadastral evidence shows a building at the later Hotel Borek location immediately beside the railway. The map does not itself name the building as Hotel Borek; that identification is strengthened by later images and industrial documentation.

Documented cartographic evidence · building identification based on comparison
Before the new station

Hotel Borek and the railway

A 2018 ČVUT account of the station's conversion states that passenger handling and railway operational offices were provisionally accommodated in rented premises in nearby Hotel Borek before the new station building was constructed. This is a valuable published account of the relationship between the hotel and railway, but the archive is still seeking the underlying railway records that would document the arrangement directly.

Published secondary account · primary railway record sought
After Strousberg · exact dates not yet established

J. Tichý and E. Brandeis are reported as successive owners of Hotel Borek

A local historical account based on the Kařez memorial book states that, after the Strousberg period, J. Tichý, described as the owner of a Prague variety theatre, acquired Hotel Borek. It says that the hotel was later purchased by E. Brandeis of the Borek factory.

The same account reports that Brandeis added a staircase, restored the first-floor guest rooms as his apartment, and altered the garden and water reservoir. This would create a direct ownership link between the factory proprietor, the hotel and the railway setting. However, the account does not provide a sufficiently established acquisition date, and the archive has not yet located the relevant deed or land-register entry. The sequence can therefore be recorded; an exact year must remain open.

Secondary local account · Tichý → Brandeis sequence reported · dates and deeds sought
1881

Roubíček and Brandeis are reported to acquire the Borek works

A later industrial history states that Roubíček and Brandeis acquired the Borek ironworks. This is the first presently identified Brandeis ownership point in the factory chronology, but the original transaction and the precise assets acquired remain to be established.

Secondary industrial history · acquisition instrument sought
1887

E. Brandeis is reported to become sole proprietor of the factory

The same historical account states that E. Brandeis purchased Roubíček’s share, converted the former rolling works into an iron foundry and increasingly centred production on machinery. This industrial transformation provides important background to the traffic and commercial activity surrounding the railway.

Secondary industrial history · company and property records sought
1907

The founder’s son is reported to take over the factory

The source says that “his son” succeeded at the Borek enterprise but does not provide the son’s complete identity. The possibility that father and son were both named Emil is a research hypothesis, not an established identification.

Reported succession · individual identity unresolved
By 1908 · continuing into the early Czechoslovak period

The Brandeis industrial enterprise operates at Borek beside the railway

A surviving 1908 business postcard signed “E. Brandeis,” later corporate records and the Brandeis–Eisenschimmel factory catalogue document a substantial industrial enterprise at Borek. The catalogue describes broad engineering and agricultural-machinery production and gives Zbiroh station as a logistical connection.

This evidence establishes an important relationship between factory operations and the station. The unresolved question is more specific: whether factory traffic, employees or commercial requirements directly caused the State Railways Directorate to build the monumental new station complex in 1911–1913. Railway traffic statistics, planning memoranda, correspondence or commercial agreements would be needed to demonstrate that causal link.

Factory–station relationship documented · construction motive remains a hypothesis
October 1911

The State Railways Directorate prepares the new station project

The heritage record dates the project to October 1911 and attributes it institutionally to the Ředitelství státních drah v Praze (State Railways Directorate in Prague). It identifies Prague builder Vendelín Hron as responsible for construction.

A separate 2018 ČVUT publication attributes the architectural design specifically to Jan Heindl. This attribution is significant because independent architectural research identifies Heindl as a revident of the Imperial-Royal State Railways and documents him designing the Vinohrady railway building at the same period. The same research also records Heindl and Hron together on signed plans for a Prague building in 1912.

The evidence therefore supports a stronger statement than simply naming Heindl as a speculative candidate: there is a published attribution of Zbiroh station to Jan Heindl. However, the heritage record itself names the State Railways Directorate rather than an individual architect, and the archive has not yet located the original Zbiroh drawings bearing Heindl's signature. The attribution remains to be confirmed from primary documentation.

Published attribution · Jan Heindl; primary-document confirmation still sought
1911–1912 · another source gives 1912–1913

The monumental late-Secession station takes shape

The heritage record dates construction of the main dispatch/residential building and associated laundry to 1911–1912. The ČVUT account gives 1912–1913. Rather than choosing between those dates without the original construction records, the archive records the discrepancy. The older technical-administration building was incorporated into and altered during the new late-Secession station complex.

Although called Zbiroh station, the complex stands in the cadastral territory of Kařez.

Dating discrepancy retained · original construction records sought
1913

Passenger life in the new station

A railway account published when passenger operation ended states that Zbiroh station had handled passengers from 1913. This is consistent with the later end of the construction range given by the ČVUT account, but does not by itself resolve whether building work began or substantially finished in 1911–1912.

Early 20th century · exact postcard date under research

Station, Hotel Borek and the earlier landscape

The postcard above preserves the physical relationship between Hotel Borek, the station and the surrounding countryside. The foreground appears low-lying and wet, while the slopes are substantially more open and sparsely wooded than today. The image establishes the visual difference; it does not establish why the tree cover was different.

1918 onward

From the Habsburg state railway system into Czechoslovakia

The political and railway administration surrounding the station changed with the creation of Czechoslovakia. The archive has not yet assembled the complete primary-document ownership and administrative chain for the building through the successive state-railway institutions.

Open research question · exact institutional ownership chain
1919

The Brandeis–Eisenschimmel concern appears in an official industrial listing

A contemporary Czech publication lists the enterprise in corporate form among industrial companies. This supplies independent evidence that a substantial recorded industrial concern was operating in the station’s immediate economic landscape.

Contemporary published listing
c. 1920

The Borek and Eisenschimmel businesses are combined

A contemporary German technical publication reports that the Borek ironworks of E. Brandeis and Franz Eisenschimmel & Co.’s agricultural-machinery factory in Roudnice were converted into a joint-stock company, with the participation of the Prague branch of the Anglo-Bank. It states capital of approximately 2.4 million crowns.

Contemporary technical source · exact corporate file still sought
1920s–1930s · exact catalogue date unresolved

“Original Borek” appears as the factory’s commercial identity

The provisionally dated surviving catalogue carries the Original Borek name and mark while presenting the factory, its machinery, worker colonies, officials’ housing and factory hotel. It identifies Kařez as the postal connection and Zbiroh station as the logistical connection. A formal corporate source from 1919 records the enterprise under the Brandeis–Eisenschimmel company name; the catalogue therefore suggests a distinction between the legal corporate identity and the later commercial or product identity used in the Czech market. The precise year in which Original Borek was adopted, and its exact legal relationship to the joint-stock company, remain unresolved.

The surviving material also places Borek within agricultural engineering: alongside cutting and processing machinery, documents promote equipment for preserving green fodder in silos and illustrate an Aurich-associated silo press. This supports a broader agricultural-machinery and storage range, but does not prove that every component was manufactured at Borek or that the company owned the underlying patents.

Primary commercial evidence · brand date, corporate relationship and manufacturing scope unresolved

Factory catalogue and product evidence · Borek–Aurich silo research · Silo documents

20th century

A working railway station

For most of the twentieth century the building continued in railway use. Photographs, plans, staffing records, alterations and other evidence will be inserted into this part of the timeline as they are identified and dated.

Research in progress
1932

The local municipality reports that factory production stopped

A later municipal history gives 1932 as the year production stopped during the Depression. This is the presently identified source for that closure date; the archive has not yet independently confirmed it through contemporary company, liquidation, employment or production records. Nor does the statement establish whether the legal company ended, the Original Borek brand disappeared, the enterprise was suspended or reorganised, or particular products and assets continued elsewhere. The factory site, operating business, legal entity and commercial brand must therefore be treated as separate histories.

Municipal secondary account · contemporary confirmation and company-register files sought
1936

The catalogue sequence documents the “Original Melichar” branding at Brandýs nad Labem

Comparative catalogue images show that by 1936 the Brandýs manufacturer was presenting the related machinery under the Original Melichar name and mark. This is the first presently documented appearance of that branding within the catalogue sequence examined by the archive. Its combination of Original with the Melichar name, following the reported acquisition of the cutter manufacturer at Borek near Kařez, raises the working hypothesis that the former Original Borek identity and product inheritance were adapted into Original Melichar.

The 1936 catalogue evidence establishes use of the later branding by that date; it does not alone establish the legal date of acquisition, prove that the word Original was deliberately transferred from Borek, show that all technology and assets moved to Brandýs, or establish when production ceased at the Borek site.

Catalogue evidence · branding documented by 1936 · transfer mechanism unresolved

Original Melichar cutter history and catalogue sequence · Commercial-transfer hypothesis

Late 1930s

The factory complex remains physically present

Historical photographs and pre-war aerial evidence show the Borek industrial complex still standing. This documents survival of buildings, not ownership or continuing production.

Photographic landscape evidence
1939–1945

The factory’s wartime use remains unresolved

No verified source presently establishes the former factory’s specific production, ownership or administrative use during the occupation. Its proximity to the railway and the documented Borek airfield makes the question important, but no operational connection should be inferred without evidence.

Unresolved
1952?

Local tradition reports a major factory fire

Local tradition places a fire at the former Borek factory around 1952. No contemporary fire report, newspaper account, municipal record or insurance file has yet confirmed the date, scale or affected buildings.

Local tradition · unconfirmed
1953 and 1969

Hotel Borek enters post-war institutional ownership

A secondary historical account states that the hotel was nationalised in 1953, later belonged to Hospodářské skladištní a výrobní družstvo Plzeň and passed to Komunální služby Zbiroh in 1969. Primary ownership records are still required.

Secondary historical account · title records sought
October 1992

Construction of the D5 motorway begins nearby

Published D5 construction history records the start of work on the Bavoryně–Mýto section in October 1992. The motorway works would substantially transform the older landscape around the station.

26 October 1995

The D5 opens through the area

The Bavoryně–Kařez and Kařez–Mýto sections of the D5 were both opened to traffic on 26 October 1995. Comparison with the early postcard provides a particularly clear illustration of the transformation of the station landscape. The precise relationship between the wet ground visible in the postcard and the later motorway footprint remains under investigation.

By 2012

České dráhy owns the station building

Contemporary reporting identifies České dráhy (Czech Railways) as the owner of the station building when its railway function was coming to an end. The archive still needs primary records documenting each institutional transfer between the original state-railway period and České dráhy.

Night of 27–28 April 2012

Passenger operation at Zbiroh ends

Passenger operation at Zbiroh ended during the night of 27–28 April 2012. The new Kařez stop entered service on 28 April and passenger trains transferred there. An SŽDC project press release identifies the first train stopping at Kařez as R 828 at 00:34. Contemporary railway reporting described the change as the end of an era for a station that had handled passengers since 1913 — almost a century.

2012

The redundant building is prepared for sale

Contemporary reports from April and July 2012 state that České dráhy intended to dispose of the now-redundant station property. The historic building had become separated from the main passenger function during the railway-corridor reconstruction.

After 2012

From railway property to a new life

The next stage of the timeline concerns the sale of the station, subsequent private ownership, preservation of the protected building and the changing relationship between the former station complex and the operational railway. Exact transfer dates and documentary evidence will be added as they are established.

Research in progress
2020

The factory catalogue is discovered during research for the station project

The project’s discovery of the surviving Brandeis–Eisenschimmel factory catalogue in 2020 materially changed the direction of the research. It supplied evidence of the company’s industrial scale, product range, commercial systems, social infrastructure and stated logistical connection with Zbiroh station.

The discovery did not by itself prove why the station was built. It did, however, make the factory–station relationship a central question rather than a peripheral possibility and opened a documentary route through company registers, railway traffic records, maps, property files and industrial publications.

Project research milestone · catalogue date and page-level record still being completed
Present

A layered historical site and research archive

The former station survives not as a single-period object but as a physical record of successive railway, industrial and landscape phases: a probable nineteenth-century railway-service building, the monumental late-Secession reconstruction, twentieth-century railway operation and the post-2012 survival of the complex after passenger closure.

Who designed Zbiroh station?

Heritage record: the October 1911 project is attributed to the State Railways Directorate in Prague and Vendelín Hron is identified as the builder.

Published individual attribution: a 2018 ČVUT account states that the station was built according to a design by architect Jan Heindl.

Independent context: Heindl was a railway and civil architect working for the Imperial-Royal State Railways. Architectural research documents his design of the Vinohrady railway building and a 1912 Prague project whose plans were signed by both Heindl and Vendelín Hron.

These sources make the Heindl attribution substantial, but the archive has not yet found the original October 1911 Zbiroh drawings or another contemporary document naming him. The page therefore records Jan Heindl as the published attribution, not yet as a primary-document-confirmed attribution. The decisive next step remains examination of the original drawings, title blocks, signatures and approval stamps.

Contribute evidence

If you have historical photographs, postcards, plans, railway documents, ownership records, architectural drawings, personal recollections or other material relating to Zbiroh station, Hotel Borek or the surrounding railway landscape, we would be interested in hearing from you. In particular, we are seeking the original 1911 station plans that could confirm or challenge the published Jan Heindl attribution, earlier records that could date the nineteenth-century technical-administration building, and documentation of the railway's use of Hotel Borek. Evidence that corrects or challenges the chronology is equally welcome.
