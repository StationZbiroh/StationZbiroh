Eugen Bárkány and two portraits once displayed at Zbiroh station

Two watercolours, two unidentified sitters, and a continuing search for their history and an appropriate permanent home.

← People and organisations

Nature of the station connection: Eugen Bárkány is not known to have lived, worked or painted at Zbiroh station. These portraits were acquired independently in Prague before the current owner bought the former station in 2014. They later hung on its walls for many years. Their presence at the station—not an established biographical connection—explains this page.

Research update — 14 October 2026

New reading of the inscriptions. With thanks to Dr. Pnina Younger, Head of Catalogue & Holdings at the National Library of Israel, whose research correspondence of 14 October 2026 provided the readings of the inscriptions on the reverse of the two portraits as “B. Führmann / 1950 / Bárkány” and “Bývalý richtar v Mereticiach” (“Former mayor of Meretice”), respectively. The identities of both sitters remain unconfirmed. The latter is not evidence that the sitter was a museum director.

Prešov Jewish Museum. Bárkány and Dr. Teodor (Tivadar) Austerlitz were leading figures in the museum's establishment in 1928; it opened in 1931. A published historical account states that the museum was closed in 1939 and its collection confiscated, with much of it safeguarded during the war. After the war the museum was not re-established. The account says the town cancelled its lease, and the collection was subsequently moved to Prague in 1952.

Bezalel, Jerusalem. The same published study, Jewish History Centres and Research Institutions in Europe (2012), p. 125, reports that the head of Bezalel in Jerusalem expressed interest in bringing the Prešov collection to Jerusalem, but the Czechoslovak state refused. The passage does not date the approach or identify the correspondent. It does not establish that Bárkány personally corresponded with Bezalel. Read page 125 of the published study.

Displacement and residence. Published biographical research reports that Bárkány and his wife were forcibly displaced from Prešov in 1951 as politically unreliable. Meretice and Smolník are recorded among their subsequent places of residence, before Bratislava in 1955. The 1953 inscription's reference to Meretice is therefore historically noteworthy, without proving where the portrait was painted.

Collection's later history. The published study reports that twelve cases of valuable objects remained unopened and unexhibited in Prague; it does not say the entire collection remained unexhibited. The collection returned to Prešov in 1993.

Provenance. The portraits were purchased at Prague's Kolbenova flea market. Their earlier ownership and route to Prague are unknown. The 1950 and 1953 dates alone do not identify the sitters or establish political connections.

Research interpretation, explicitly unproven. Bárkány's reported displacement may be examined in the context of communist-era removals of people deemed politically unreliable, sometimes discussed under Action B (Akcia B). Whether contacts with Israel or the proposed Bezalel transfer contributed to his treatment is unknown; original archival correspondence and administrative records are needed.

Further sources: Prešov museum history; Slovak Jewish Heritage biography; National Library of Israel correspondence, 14 October 2026. See also the separate research dossier.

Who was Eugen Bárkány?

Eugen Bárkány (1885–1967) was a Prešov-born building engineer, artist, collector and pioneering advocate of Jewish heritage preservation in Slovakia. He helped establish the Jewish Museum in Prešov in 1928. During the Holocaust he survived in hiding; in the early communist period he was expelled from Prešov in 1951. His collecting and documentation work continued in Bratislava. His legacy includes important Judaica collections preserved in Prešov and Bratislava.

The portraits

Image links will display after the two JPEG files are uploaded to the repository main folder.

The station owner purchased two framed watercolour portraits attributed to Bárkány at the Kolbenova flea market in Prague, probably in 2012 or 2013. One is dated 1950 and depicts an older, white-haired man with a moustache. The other is dated 1953 and shows a seated middle-aged man. Both were displayed at the former Zbiroh station after its acquisition in 2014. There is no evidence that either picture was made at the station or that either sitter had a connection to Zbiroh.

In October 2026, the works were removed from their frames for closer examination, revealing writing on their reverse sides. In correspondence dated 14 October 2026, Dr. Pnina Younger of the National Library of Israel read the 1950 inscription as “B. Führmann / 1950 / Bárkány” and the 1953 inscription as “Bývalý richtar v Mereticiach” (“Former mayor of Meretice”). These readings supersede the earlier provisional transcriptions; neither sitter has been securely identified. The inscriptions concern different portraits and different unidentified people; the possible occupational description on the 1953 work must not be transferred to the 1950 sitter.

These clues have not yet established the sitters' names, religious identities, occupations or fates. The dates place the portraits in the difficult postwar and early communist period, but do not prove that the sitters were victims of persecution or dispossession.

A coincidence encountered in the research

During an earlier investigation based on a now-superseded provisional reading “Fischmann” on the 1950 portrait, the researcher encountered musician Robert Fischmann, who was listed to perform in September 2026 at the Orthodox Synagogue in Prešov—the synagogue where part of Bárkány's Judaica collection is exhibited. This lead is retained solely as a record of the research process: Dr. Pnina Younger's subsequent reading “B. Führmann” gives no reason to associate this musician with the sitter; the concert listing alone does not confirm that the performance took place.

Historical context: Action B and the Slánský Trial, 1951–1953

These portraits date from a period of overlapping political repression in communist Czechoslovakia. Two distinct developments provide important context: the Slánský Trial (November 1952), a show trial marked by antisemitic and anti-Zionist accusations, and Action B (Akcia B / Akce B, 1952–1953), a campaign of forced removals of people classified as politically unreliable from cities to other locations. The Slovak Nation's Memory Institute documents the expulsions, including nearly 700 families from Bratislava. These were different forms of repression, not one coordinated operation. Read the Museum of Victims of Communism's account of Action B.

Bárkány and his wife were reportedly forced to leave Prešov in the second half of 1951, before the main documented 1952–1953 Action B campaign. This makes his displacement relevant to the broader history of political expulsions, but it is not yet established that his individual removal was formally part of Action B. His later residence in Meretice is especially interesting alongside the 1953 portrait's inscription referring to a former mayor of that village. Neither this inscription nor the coincidence in dates demonstrates a direct connection to the Slánský Trial.

The dates of these portraits frame a particularly fraught period in Czechoslovak history. The 1950 work predates the November 1952 Slánský Trial; the 1953 work follows it. The trial of Rudolf Slánský and thirteen other senior Communist officials took place from 20 to 27 November 1952. Eleven defendants were executed; eleven of the fourteen were of Jewish origin. Antisemitic and anti-Zionist accusations were central to the proceedings. This wider history is documented, but no evidence currently links either unidentified sitter to the trial or its victims.

The 1953 portrait's reverse has since been read by Dr. Pnina Younger as “Bývalý richtar v Mereticiach” (“Former mayor of Meretice”). This identifies a former municipal role, not a directorship. The name of the sitter, the dates of his office and any connection to the Slánský Trial remain unknown.

Czechoslovakia's early support for Israel gave way to serious Cold War diplomatic tensions. Anti-Zionist rhetoric in the trial further damaged relations; the countries did not formally break diplomatic relations until 1967. Bárkány's own experience, including his 1951 expulsion from Prešov, raises questions about the constraints facing Jewish cultural figures after the war. His personal reasons for remaining in Czechoslovakia are not established by these paintings.

Comparative research question: Do securely attributed Bárkány portraits from 1950–1953 show consistent patterns in their inscriptions, and can surviving archives identify either sitter? The artist's signature, date, inscriptions, provenance and technique should be assessed together rather than treating historical coincidences as proof.

Borek factory and the year 1952: a separate hypothesis

Research into the former Borek ironworks, historically associated with the Jewish Brandeis family and the Brandeis–Eisenschimmel concern, suggests major physical changes in the mid-twentieth century. Earlier comparisons of aerial photographs from 1938, 1946 and 1951 show that the site's buildings changed substantially over time; a fire in 1952 has been reported in the research, but its primary documentation and the precise demolition chronology still require confirmation.

Hypothesis only: If substantial demolition occurred during or after the antisemitic political campaign surrounding the 1952 Slánský Trial, could hostility toward the factory's former Jewish-associated ownership have influenced decisions about the buildings or their symbolic treatment? At present there is no evidence establishing such a motive, a decision-maker, or a causal link. Coincidence in dates cannot distinguish ideological destruction from fire damage, safety concerns, state industrial planning, redundancy, reuse or other explanations. Nor is it established that the entire former factory was demolished in 1952.

Evidence needed includes dated demolition permits and plans, the original 1952 fire report, national-enterprise and municipal records, photographs after 1951, cadastral changes, and any contemporary statements about the site's former owners. This question belongs to the Borek factory chronology as well as the broader study of Jewish industrial heritage; it is included here as a comparative research lead, not as an explanation for the portraits.

What happens next?

The owner hopes that the portraits may eventually enter the care of Yad Vashem or the National Library of Israel, subject to agreement and appropriate conservation arrangements. Neither destination has been finalised. The aim is to preserve the works, identify the sitters if possible, and make their story accessible to future researchers.

Images of the fronts, reverse inscriptions and associated correspondence are held in the owner's research material. They are not reproduced here until a suitable, verified set of images has been prepared for publication.
