Industrial formation and the first railway

1862

The Prague–Plzeň railway creates the transport context

The line opened in 1862, creating the framework within which the later industrial and settlement histories developed. The precise status of stopping or loading facilities at this particular site in 1862 remains unresolved.

Context established · local arrangement unresolved Station chronology

1866

The financial machinery behind the sale begins to move

The Austrian state pledged Zbiroh with other Crown properties as security for a 60-million-gulden loan from the Allgemeine Österreichische Boden-Credit-Anstalt. This did not yet transfer ownership to Strousberg, but it is the earliest identified step in the financial chain that made the estate’s later sale possible.

Published reconstruction · original loan instrument sought How Strousberg bought Zbiroh · Property rights

20 June–21 July 1868

The state authorises the sale and transfers Zbiroh to two financiers

The sale law was signed on 20 June 1868. On 21 July, Samuel Simundt and Vinzenz Kirchmayer bought the estate from the state for 6.5 million gulden. Their short period as purchasers forms the legal and financial bridge between state ownership and Strousberg’s acquisition.

Published reconstruction · sale instruments sought Purchase sequence and financing

31 December 1868

Strousberg acquires the Zbiroh estate through a heavily financed transaction

Simundt and Kirchmayer resold the estate to Bethel Henry Strousberg for 9.4 million gulden. His obligation comprised 6.4 million gulden payable in cash instalments and assumption of a 3-million-gulden mortgage. The acquisition created the property base from which Strousberg attempted the rapid industrial transformation of the small Zbiroh–Borek area: factories, transport links, workers’ housing and supporting facilities were subsequently developed as parts of one connected system.

The exact deposit and instalment schedule remain to be established from the original contract and land-register records.

c. 1871

Hotel Borek is built within Strousberg’s industrial settlement

The official Kařez history, the National Heritage Institute and an account based on the Kařez memorial book associate Hotel Borek no. 39 with Strousberg’s development of the Borek works and settlement. The precise construction contract and original ownership instrument have not yet been located.

A building stands at the later Hotel Borek site

A cadastral record shows a building beside the railway at the later hotel location. The map does not name it Hotel Borek; that identification is strengthened by comparison with later industrial documentation explicitly describing the building as the factory hotel and officials’ social house.

Collapse, reporting and redistribution

1 March 1875

The Goldschmidt assessment values the Zbirow industrial system

Chief engineer Franz Rziha’s report for H. v. Goldschmidt & Co. includes the Borek works and documents a proposed refinancing relationship. It does not prove that the bank acquired the factory or that it was related to the later Goldschmied parties.

Primary report Zbirow report · Memoir context · The Economist

From 28 October 1875

The feared riot never occurs—but troops stay in Hotel Borek for a month

The Prague Commercial Court opened bankruptcy proceedings over Strousberg’s Austrian property on 28 October 1875. Barbora Turková’s reconstruction, citing the Zbiroh town chronicle, records that an infantry battalion was called from Plzeň and Rokycany because the authorities feared unrest among unpaid workers. Zbiroh was quiet: contemporary reporting states that no violent conflict occurred and no weapons were used. Even so, the troops moved to Borek and lived in the hotel for an entire month.

The episode places Hotel Borek directly inside the social history of Strousberg’s collapse. The available evidence establishes military accommodation there for approximately one month, but does not yet establish that the hotel permanently closed when the troops arrived.

Chronicle and contemporary press evidence Hotel Borek evidence · Turková thesis, pp. 70 and 73

After 28 October 1875 · exact date unresolved

The bankruptcy administrator sells Hotel Borek’s furniture and equipment

Turková records that bankruptcy administrator Dr Josef Tragy progressively sold railway material, equipment from Strousberg’s enterprises, and the furniture and equipment of Hotel Borek. This is separate from the troops’ stay and provides evidence that the hotel’s contents were administered within the disposal of Strousberg-connected assets.

The sale strongly supports the hotel’s place in Strousberg’s property system, but an inventory sale alone does not prove legal title to the building or parcel. The original bankruptcy inventory, sale notices and land-register entry are still required, and the precise date of the hotel sale has not yet been established.

Assets and names reappear in new records

Auction reports and 1879 property evidence provide fixed points after the collapse. The relationship to the earlier Goldschmidt banking house remains unproven.

Brandeis industry and the new station

1908

A business postcard is signed “E. Brandeis”

The object anchors the Brandeis name locally. Attribution to a particular individual or generation requires comparison with civil, company and residence records.

1911–1913

The new Zbiroh station is designed, built and enters passenger use

The State Railways Directorate project is dated October 1911; heritage evidence dates construction to 1911–1912, another source gives 1912–1913, and a railway account states that passenger use began in 1913. The original planning files remain essential to explaining the building’s scale and resolving the dating.

Documented range · dating discrepancy retained Station history

1918–c. 1920

The Brandeis and Eisenschimmel enterprises are combined at Borek

Industrial-topography research records a 1918 agreement between Brandeis and Franz Eisenschimmel & Co. to transfer agricultural-machinery production from Roudnice nad Labem to Borek. A 1919 Czech listing and a contemporary German technical publication then document the combined company.

Airfield, occupation and post-war change

c. 1930–1938

Borek field airfield develops beside the industrial landscape

Military-history research places the field airfield’s establishment around 1930. It served aviation–artillery cooperation connected with the Brdy training area. On 12 July 1932 an Aero Ap-11.54 crashed at Borek; during mobilisation, Letka 4 operated here with Letov Š-328 observation aircraft from 24 September to 22 October 1938.

Published military chronology · aerial evidence Borek Airfield timeline, photographs and sources · 1938 ČÚZK aerial survey

1932–1941

Reported closure and continuing Borek-derived production diverge

Local and heritage accounts place the end of foundry activity at Borek around 1931–1932, while Melichar catalogues and factory negatives trace Borek-derived agricultural machines through the 1930s and into 1941. This supports product-line continuity elsewhere, but does not yet establish the legal survival, ownership or operating status of the Brandeis–Eisenschimmel company.

1938–1946

The Borek factory site becomes substantially larger

Comparison of the 1938 and 1946 state aerial surveys shows a changed and enlarged industrial footprint beside the former airfield, with additional structures on land that appears open in the earlier image. Because most of the interval falls within the occupation, this is important evidence of occupation-era physical development. The photographs do not by themselves date each building, identify its use, or prove who controlled the site or what was produced there.

Aerial-survey evidence Photographs, survey identifiers and full evidence note · 1946 ČÚZK aerial survey

1939–1946

Airfield infrastructure disappears as the factory footprint changes

Structures visible around the airfield before the war appear absent from the 1946 survey. The visual sequence establishes landscape change, not its cause: abandonment, dismantling, repurposing and return to agriculture remain open possibilities, and no direct connection with the neighbouring factory expansion has yet been proved.

Visual change documented · cause unresolved Borek Airfield evidence

30 July 1940

The full Brandeis–Eisenschimmel name remains in public circulation

The Museum of Securities catalogues a Borek factory item under the full company name and records that it was sent on 30 July 1940. This proves circulation of the name and image after the occupation began, but an older postcard could have been posted later; it does not alone establish production, ownership or legal registration in 1940.

Museum catalogue record T1278 Museum of Securities record · Brandeis–Eisenschimmel factory images

1941–1942

Emil Brandeis is persecuted and deported

The archive’s biographical research follows the Borek-connected Emil Brandeis, born in Kařez in 1868, who was deported to Terezín on 13 July 1942 and subsequently to Treblinka. His identity must not be confused with another Emil Brandeis, born in 1873, recorded in a separate Holocaust victim entry.

Victim and biographical records Identity, evidence and remembrance

5 July 1943–16 October 1944

Dr Valtr Eisenschimmel survives more than a year in Terezín before deportation to Auschwitz

The chemist and former specialist assistant at the German Technical University in Prague was deported with his wife Helena and young son Tomáš. A Mladá Boleslav family history reports that his status as a specialist helped him survive in the ghetto for more than a year. His precise work there is not yet documented by the sources consulted.

Victim record · secondary biography Valtr Eisenschimmel

1953

Hotel Borek is nationalised

A secondary account based on the Kařez memorial book states that the hotel was nationalised in 1953. It later belonged to Hospodářské skladištní a výrobní družstvo Plzeň and, from 1969, to Komunální služby Zbiroh; the complete primary ownership chain remains to be reconstructed.

September 2026

An incorrect AI answer leads to a documented family connection

An earlier ChatGPT answer claimed that Dr Walter Eisenschimmel lived in Kařez. Subsequent checking did not verify that claim and found that part of the answer’s chronology was wrong. The question nevertheless opened a productive research path connecting Valtr (Walter), Ludwig and Franz Eisenschimmel with the company that transferred agricultural-machine production from Roudnice nad Labem to Borek.
