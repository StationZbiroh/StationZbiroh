Who was Emil Brandeis?

A reconstruction of the Brandeis family’s connection with the Borek ironworks and machinery factory, and an open investigation into the identity of the Emil Brandeis named in surviving industrial and Holocaust-era records.

Source photograph 1 · Source photograph 2 · Factory image archive

What is established, and what is not

Surviving sources repeatedly connect the Brandeis name, and specifically the form “E. Brandeis”, with the Borek works. A secondary industrial history states that Roubíček and Brandeis acquired the works in 1881, that E. Brandeis became sole proprietor in 1887, and that his son took over in 1907. A separate contemporary technical publication later identifies the Borek concern as the firm of E. Brandeis when it was combined with Franz Eisenschimmel & Co.

It was entirely plausible in this period for a son to bear the same given name as his father. This raises a serious working hypothesis that the records may concern an elder and younger Emil Brandeis. That hypothesis is not yet proven. The archive therefore separates documented company history from the identification of individual family members.

Timeline: the Brandeis family and Borek

1881
Secondary source

A historical account of the former Strousberg industrial complex records the acquisition of the Borek ironworks by Roubíček and Brandeis.
1887
Secondary source
E. Brandeis becomes sole proprietor.
The same account states that E. Brandeis bought Roubíček’s share and converted the former rolling works into an iron foundry, with production increasingly centred on machinery.
1907
Key identity clue
“His son” takes over the factory.
The source does not give the son’s full name. This succession is one of the central clues in the current research. The possibility that both father and son were named Emil remains a hypothesis pending company-register or genealogical confirmation.
1908
Documented object
A business postcard is signed “E. Brandeis”.
The surviving object confirms continued use of the name in the factory’s commercial world, but the signature has not yet been assigned conclusively to the elder or younger generation.
1919
Contemporary listing
The Brandeis–Eisenschimmel concern appears in an official industrial listing.
A Czech publication lists the enterprise in corporate form among industrial companies of the period.
c. 1920
Contemporary source
The Borek firm is combined with Franz Eisenschimmel & Co.
A contemporary German technical publication reports the conversion of the Borek ironworks of the firm E. Brandeis and the Roudnice agricultural-machinery business Franz Eisenschimmel & Co. into a joint-stock company, with capital stated as 2.4 million crowns.
1920s–1930s
Primary-source catalogue
A broad industrial catalogue documents the company.
The surviving catalogue records a substantial range of foundry, engineering and agricultural-machinery products and is one of the central primary sources for reconstructing the Borek enterprise. Open the factory catalogue page.
1932
Unresolved
Production is reported to have stopped during the Depression, but liquidation has not been established.
A later historical account says that operations ceased during the economic crisis and that the site was subsequently used for grain storage. That may reflect a genuine closure caused by Depression-era conditions and deflated prices. However, the archive has not located a company-register entry, liquidation file, dissolution notice or comparable primary document proving that the company was formally liquidated at this point. A possible alternative is that the business was consolidated, reorganised or continued in another legal or corporate form. This remains an open research question. At present, the claim of liquidation is known principally from the local municipal narrative and has not yet been independently documented.

A possible Emil Brandeis: identity under investigation

The Terezín Initiative Institute’s Holocaust database contains a record for an Emil Brandeis, born 29 October 1873. It gives his last residence as Prague XI, Kříšťanova 9/683, and records his deportation on 31 October 1941 from Prague to Łódź on Transport D, no. 596. The record states that he was murdered.

This is a securely documented individual, but the archive has not yet established that he was the Emil Brandeis associated with the Borek factory. His record is therefore presented as a research candidate rather than incorporated into the factory biography as settled fact.

The father-and-son hypothesis

The industrial chronology creates a very specific question. If an E. Brandeis was sole proprietor by 1887 and his son succeeded him in 1907, there were clearly two generations involved in the business. Naming a son after his father was not unusual, so an Emil Brandeis senior / Emil Brandeis junior sequence is historically plausible. The repeated appearance of “E. Brandeis” in company material makes this worth pursuing, but plausibility is not identification.

The strongest next evidence would be a commercial-register file, succession record, birth or marriage record, or another company document naming both generations together.
