Who was Emil Brandeis?

A reconstruction of the Brandeis family’s connection with the Borek ironworks and machinery factory, and an open investigation into the identity of the Emil Brandeis named in surviving industrial and Holocaust-era records.

Source photograph 1 · Source photograph 2 · Factory image archive

What is established, and what is not

Surviving sources repeatedly connect the Brandeis name, and specifically the form “E. Brandeis”, with the Borek works. A secondary industrial history states that Roubíček and Brandeis acquired the works in 1881, that E. Brandeis became sole proprietor in 1887, and that his son took over in 1907. A separate contemporary technical publication later identifies the Borek concern as the firm of E. Brandeis when it was combined with Franz Eisenschimmel & Co.

It was entirely plausible in this period for a son to bear the same given name as his father. This raises a serious working hypothesis that the records may concern an elder and younger Emil Brandeis. That hypothesis is not yet proven. The archive therefore separates documented company history from the identification of individual family members.

Timeline: the Brandeis family and Borek

1875–1881
Open question
What happened to the Borek works after Strousberg’s collapse?
Strousberg’s financial system collapsed in 1875, while the next ownership point currently identified in the Borek chronology is the acquisition by Roubíček and Brandeis in 1881. The intervening years remain to be reconstructed: the works may have been administered by creditors, operated under another owner or manager, reduced in activity, or partly dormant.
1881
Secondary source
Roubíček and Brandeis acquire the Borek works.
A historical account of the former Strousberg industrial complex records the acquisition of the Borek ironworks by Roubíček and Brandeis.
1887
Secondary source
E. Brandeis becomes sole proprietor.
The same account states that E. Brandeis bought Roubíček’s share and converted the former rolling works into an iron foundry, with production increasingly centred on machinery.
1907
Key identity clue
“His son” takes over the factory.
The source does not give the son’s full name. This succession is one of the central clues in the current research. The possibility that both father and son were named Emil remains a hypothesis pending company-register or genealogical confirmation.
1908
Documented object
A business postcard is signed “E. Brandeis”.
The surviving object confirms continued use of the name in the factory’s commercial world, but the signature has not yet been assigned conclusively to the elder or younger generation.
1911–1912
Documented
The present Zbiroh station complex is built.
The heritage record dates the station complex to 1911–1912, as part of the doubling of the Prague–Plzeň railway. The design by the State Railways Directorate in Prague is dated October 1911, and the complex was built by Prague builder Vendelín Hron. This places the construction of the surviving station directly within the period when the Brandeis factory was operating nearby. Source: Památkový katalog.
1919
Contemporary listing
The Brandeis–Eisenschimmel concern appears in an official industrial listing.
A Czech publication lists the enterprise in corporate form among industrial companies of the period.
c. 1920
Contemporary source
The Borek firm is combined with Franz Eisenschimmel & Co.
A contemporary German technical publication reports the conversion of the Borek ironworks of the firm E. Brandeis and the Roudnice agricultural-machinery business Franz Eisenschimmel & Co. into a joint-stock company, with capital stated as 2.4 million crowns.
1920s–1930s
Primary-source catalogue
A broad industrial catalogue documents the company.
The surviving catalogue records a substantial range of foundry, engineering and agricultural-machinery products and is one of the central primary sources for reconstructing the Borek enterprise. Open the factory catalogue page.
1932
Open question
Did the company actually cease to exist?
A later local account says production stopped during the Depression, which is entirely plausible given the economic conditions and falling prices. However, the archive has not yet found a liquidation file, dissolution notice, company-register entry or other primary record showing that the company itself was formally wound up. The open question is therefore whether the Borek company was liquidated, whether production was suspended, or whether the enterprise was reorganised, consolidated or continued under another corporate structure.

The factory after 1932

The surviving evidence becomes fragmentary after the reported cessation of production. The physical survival of the industrial complex must therefore be distinguished from the legal status of the company and from any continuing industrial activity.

Late 1930s
Photographic evidence
The Borek factory complex was still physically present before the Second World War.
Historical photographs and pre-war aerial evidence show the industrial complex remaining substantially intact. This establishes the survival of the buildings, but does not by itself establish who owned the enterprise or whether manufacturing continued.
Pre-war / WWII
Documented landscape · unresolved relationship
A military airfield existed at Borek in the same wider landscape.
Research correspondence and aviation sources document the former Borek field airfield, used by Czechoslovak military aviation before the war. Material concerning autogyro testing also forms part of the research trail, although the precise relationship between testing at Borek and Prague-Letňany requires careful distinction. Whether the airfield had any operational connection with the factory or railway remains an open question.
1939–1945
Unresolved
What was the factory used for during the occupation?
No verified source has yet established the factory’s specific wartime production, ownership or administrative use. The archive therefore leaves this period open rather than inferring activity from the survival of the buildings.
After 1945
Research in progress
Post-war ownership and use remain to be reconstructed.
The next questions concern ownership, possible nationalisation, storage use, industrial reuse and the administrative history of the property.
1952?
Local tradition · unconfirmed
Did the factory burn down in 1952?
Local tradition places a major fire at the former Borek factory around 1952. No contemporary fire report, newspaper account, municipal record, insurance record or other primary confirmation has yet been located. The date, scale of the event and the buildings affected therefore remain open questions.

A possible Emil Brandeis: identity under investigation

The Terezín Initiative Institute’s Holocaust database contains a record for an Emil Brandeis, born 29 October 1873. It gives his last residence as Prague XI, Kříšťanova 9/683, and records his deportation on 31 October 1941 from Prague to Łódź on Transport D, no. 596. The record states that he was murdered.

This is a securely documented individual, but the archive has not yet established that he was the Emil Brandeis associated with the Borek factory. His record is therefore presented as a research candidate rather than incorporated into the factory biography as settled fact.

The father-and-son hypothesis

The industrial chronology creates a very specific question. If an E. Brandeis was sole proprietor by 1887 and his son succeeded him in 1907, there were clearly two generations involved in the business. Naming a son after his father was not unusual, so an Emil Brandeis senior / Emil Brandeis junior sequence is historically plausible. The repeated appearance of “E. Brandeis” in company material makes this worth pursuing, but plausibility is not identification.

The strongest next evidence would be a commercial-register file, succession record, birth or marriage record, or another company document naming both generations together.

Questions that remain

If the Emil Brandeis recorded in the Holocaust-era sources can ultimately be identified with the Borek industrial family, he may once have known the answers to many of the questions that surviving records now leave unresolved. His murder, and the destruction and dispersal of families, businesses and records during this period, mean that the history must now be reconstructed cautiously from the evidence that survives. These questions guide the continuing investigation:

  1. What happened to the Borek works after Strousberg’s collapse? Who owned, administered or operated them before the documented acquisition by Roubíček and Brandeis in 1881?
  2. Who exactly was Roubíček? Was he an industrial operator, investor, creditor, attorney, representative or intermediary, and did he represent other financial or family interests?
  3. What was the Goldschmidt connection? Could archival records establish whether younger members of the family held financial or beneficial interests while other people managed or represented those interests?
  4. What exactly did Roubíček and Brandeis acquire in 1881? Did the transaction concern the property, an operating enterprise, machinery, debts, shares, or a combination of these?
  5. What changed in 1887? Why did E. Brandeis acquire Roubíček’s share, and did this represent a change in the ownership, financing or active management of the works?
  6. Who was “E. Brandeis,” and who was the son who succeeded him in 1907? Were father and son both named Emil?
  7. What exactly happened to the company around 1920? How were the Borek and Eisenschimmel interests combined, and who owned and controlled the resulting joint-stock company?
  8. What really happened in 1932? Production is reported to have stopped during the Depression, but was the company liquidated, suspended, reorganised, consolidated, or continued under another corporate structure?
  9. Who owned and used the factory in the late 1930s and during the Second World War? Photographic evidence indicates that the factory complex remained physically present in the pre-war period, but its ownership and use require further documentation.
  10. What was happening at the nearby Borek airfield? Surviving aviation material and correspondence establish the airfield as an important part of the wider pre-war and wartime landscape. Was there any relationship between the airfield, railway and industrial complex?
  11. What happened to the factory property after 1945? Its ownership, possible nationalisation, storage use, industrial reuse and administrative history remain to be reconstructed.
  12. Did the factory burn in 1952? Local tradition records a major fire, but a contemporary fire report, newspaper account, municipal record, insurance record or other primary confirmation has not yet been located. What burned, and when?
